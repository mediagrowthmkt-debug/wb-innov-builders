How the typical Reading house decides what kind of deck you can build

Before we talk about boards or colors, we look at the house, because in Reading the house dictates the deck. According to housing data compiled by NeighborhoodScout, roughly 37 percent of Reading's homes were built between 1940 and 1969, about 27 percent predate 1940, and around 21 percent went up between 1970 and 1999. Single-family detached homes make up about 71 percent of the housing units in town. In practice that means three families of houses show up again and again on our estimates.

Post-war capes and ranches

The capes and ranches from the 1940s through the 1960s usually sit low to the ground, with a back door only a step or two above grade. These homes rarely need a tall deck. They need a low platform that lines up with the threshold, drains well, and does not trap moisture against old sill plates. The challenge is often the rim joist: on a house of that age, it may never have been designed to carry a ledger, so we check it carefully before we bolt anything to it.

Older colonials and Victorians near the center of town

The pre-1940 houses closer to Reading Square and along the older streets tend to have taller foundations, a kitchen floor several feet above the yard, and trim details worth respecting. A deck here often becomes a raised structure with a real stair run, and the design question is how to keep it from looking like an add-on hung off the back of a period home.

Splits, garrisons and later colonials

Houses from the 1970s through the 1990s, including split-levels and garrison colonials, frequently have a slider on the upper level and a walkout or partial walkout below. That is where second-story decks, under-deck space and multi-level layouts make sense.

Low platform decks for Reading ranches and capes

A low deck sounds simple, and it is where a lot of Reading decks go wrong. When the framing sits a foot or less above the soil, air barely moves underneath, leaves and pine needles collect, and the joists stay damp for weeks after a storm. Many of the shaded, tree-lined lots around Reading make that worse.

On these builds we take a few specific steps:

  • Ground prep first: we clear organic material, grade the soil away from the house, and lay landscape fabric and stone under the frame so the space stays drier and weeds stay out.
  • Ground-contact rated lumber: framing that sits close to grade gets lumber treated for ground contact, not the above-ground grade that is fine on a taller deck.
  • Joist tape on the framing: a strip of butyl tape over the top of each joist keeps water from sitting in the fastener holes, which matters more when the frame dries slowly.
  • Threshold math: we set the deck surface just below the door sill so snow melt does not run into the house, a detail that is easy to miss on a ranch with a low back door.

A one-step platform off a Reading cape is often the most affordable way to add real outdoor living space, and because it is close to the ground, many of them do not need a guard at all. They still need a permit when the Town's checklist calls for one, and we confirm that during the estimate.

Second-story and walkout decks on Reading colonials and splits

When the kitchen or family room is eight to ten feet above the yard, the deck becomes a structural project in the full sense. The ledger has to be through-bolted to the house framing, flashed so water cannot get behind it, and backed up with lateral load connectors where the design calls for them. Posts need to be sized for the height, beams need to span correctly, and the stair needs a landing and a guard that works for the whole run.

These are also the decks where the space underneath deserves a plan. On a walkout split in Reading, we can leave the underside open for storage, add lattice or vertical board skirting, or install an under-deck drainage system that keeps a patio below dry. Deciding that up front changes where posts go, so it is part of the design conversation, not an afterthought.

Building a deck near the Ipswich River, the Aberjona headwaters or a Reading wetland

Reading has more water on the map than most people realize. The Ipswich River forms the town's northern boundary and runs through the Reading Town Forest. The Aberjona River starts in Reading, and the Town has invested in the Aberjona River Headwaters Trail through the Maillet Conservation Area between Lowell Street and Willow Street. There are also numerous vernal pools on conservation land and a Town wetland map that shows how many backyards sit near a regulated resource.

According to the Reading Conservation Division, the Conservation Commission regulates work within 100 feet of a wetland, lake or pond, within 200 feet of a river or stream, and within the 100-year floodplain. The Town specifically lists decks, patios and additions as projects that need prior approval when they fall inside those areas. Reading also adopted updated local Wetlands Protection Regulations in 2023.

What this means in practice for your deck:

  • We check the map before we design. If your lot is near the Town Forest, a brook, a pond or a mapped wetland, we look at it at the first visit so the design, the schedule and the budget account for a conservation filing.
  • Footing work is the sensitive part. Digging holes, stockpiling soil and moving equipment are what the Commission cares about, so we plan erosion controls and keep spoils away from the resource area.
  • Timing changes. A conservation review can add weeks before the building permit is finalized, especially if a hearing is required. We build that into the calendar instead of promising a start date we cannot keep.
  • Your lot size may already reflect this. Reading's zoning bylaw only credits wetland area toward minimum lot size when enough upland remains (12,000 square feet in the S-15 and S-20 districts, 20,000 in S-40), which is a clue that many Reading lots have less usable yard than their total acreage suggests.

Wet spring soil near these areas also affects construction. Yards close to the river or a wetland often stay saturated into May, so we sometimes schedule footing work for a drier window and use footing systems that suit soft ground.

Setbacks and lot coverage on Reading's S-15, S-20 and S-40 lots

Most single-family homes in Reading sit in one of three zoning districts: S-15, S-20 or S-40, named for their minimum lot sizes of 15,000, 20,000 and 40,000 square feet. The Town's Table of Dimensional Controls sets the same yard requirements across all three for a one- or two-family home: a 20-foot front yard, a 15-foot side yard, a 20-foot rear yard, and a maximum building coverage of 25 percent of the lot.

A deck that stays comfortably inside those lines is straightforward. Trouble starts on narrow lots, corner lots and houses that were already built close to a side line decades ago. The bylaw also has separate rules for accessory structures within required side and rear yards, including a five-foot minimum from the lot line and a cap on how much of that yard they can occupy. How a specific deck is classified is the Building Commissioner's call, so we lay the design on your certified plot plan early and confirm the approach before you commit to a size. If a design would need zoning relief, you hear that from us at the design stage, not from the Town after you have paid a deposit.

Decks inside the West Street and Summer Avenue historic districts

Reading has two local historic districts, West Street and Summer Avenue, overseen by a five-member Historic District Commission. The Commission's design guidelines list the exterior items it most often reviews when they can be seen from a public way, and outside stairs and roof decks are on that list. Terraces and walks that sit substantially at grade are exempt, and ordinary maintenance and repair is exempt as long as it does not change the materials, design or size of the feature.

For a homeowner in either district, that usually works out like this: replacing a few rotted boards in kind on a deck hidden behind the house is maintenance, while a new raised deck with a visible stair, or a rebuild that changes the look of a structure seen from the street, may need Commission review before the building permit. We check your address against the Town's district property lists and, when review applies, prepare drawings and material samples that respect the house. Painted wood rails, simple square balusters and trim that echoes the existing house tend to fit these streets better than a glossy all-composite system.

Where Reading deck budgets tend to grow

Most cost surprises on a Reading deck come from the same handful of places. We price for them honestly or call them out as allowances so nobody is caught off guard.

  • The rim joist behind the ledger. On a 60- or 70-year-old house, the band board behind an old deck is often stained, soft or split from years of trapped water. It has to be repaired before a new ledger goes on.
  • Lead paint on older trim. Reading homes built before 1978 may have lead paint on the siding or trim we disturb when we remove a ledger. We follow lead-safe work practices, which adds time and containment.
  • Old footings that do not qualify. Shallow or undersized piers from an older deck usually cannot be reused and have to be replaced.
  • Tree roots and ledge. Mature trees are one of the best things about Reading yards, but big roots or buried stone can move a footing location or require different equipment.
  • Engineering or conservation requirements. A stamped plan or an erosion control plan near a wetland is a real line item, and we identify the need early.

Reuse the old footings or start over?

It is a fair question, because reusing a structure saves money on paper. On the Reading decks we open up, the answer depends on what we find:

  1. We expose the footings. If they are poured concrete piers that reach proper frost depth, are sized for the load, and sit where the new framing plan needs them, they may stay.
  2. We check the posts and beam. Posts buried in soil or set directly in concrete often rot at the base. Those get replaced even when the pier is fine.
  3. We look behind the ledger. Missing flashing, nails instead of bolts, or a ledger fastened through siding means the connection comes apart and gets rebuilt correctly.
  4. We compare against current code. A deck that was acceptable when it was built may not match what the inspector expects today, particularly at the ledger, the guards and the stairs.

In our experience most Reading decks older than about 25 years end up as full rebuilds, which is exactly what our Reading portfolio project was. When a frame is genuinely sound, we will tell you and price a resurface instead.

Railings, stairs and connections: what inspectors look at hardest

On a deck inspection, the boards are the least interesting part. Inspectors spend their time on the pieces that keep people from falling and keep the deck attached to the house. We build those to the Massachusetts State Building Code, which is based on the International Residential Code:

  • Guards: required where the walking surface is more than 30 inches above grade, at a minimum height of 36 inches for residential decks, with openings small enough to stop a 4-inch sphere.
  • Stairs: consistent riser heights and tread depths from top to bottom, a graspable handrail on stairs with four or more risers, and a solid landing at the bottom instead of a stringer resting on soil.
  • Ledger: bolted, not nailed, into solid house framing, with flashing that sheds water over the top and away from the rim.
  • Posts and beams: proper post-to-beam hardware and bracing sized for the height, especially on second-story Reading decks.
  • Stair lighting: not always required, but on a Reading winter evening a lit stair is one of the cheapest safety upgrades you can add.

For a closer look at interior and exterior stair work, see our guide to stair remodeling in Reading.

Snow, freeze-thaw and footing depth north of Boston

Reading sits about 16 miles north of downtown Boston, and its winters bring the full New England cycle: snow that piles on a deck for weeks, rain on top of it, and repeated freezing and thawing. Every part of the structure has to respect that.

Footings go below the frost line, which in eastern Massachusetts means roughly four feet down, so frost heave cannot lift a post in February and drop it in April. Framing is sized for the snow load the code requires, not just for a summer party. We space and fasten boards so meltwater drains through instead of pooling, and we keep the deck surface below the door sill so drifting snow does not become a leak into the kitchen. If you plan to shovel your deck, tell us: it changes our advice on board material and on fastener style.

Problems we find on older Reading decks

These are the issues we see most often when we are called to look at an existing deck in town, usually one built by a previous owner or a crew that is long gone:

  • Ledgers nailed through siding with no flashing, leaving a line of rot along the house rim.
  • Posts sunk directly in the ground, soft at the base after a couple of decades in Reading's damp soil.
  • Stairs with uneven risers because the grade under them settled over time.
  • Guards added after the fact and screwed to the outside of the rim, which loosen as soon as someone leans on them.
  • Undersized beams that sag in the middle of a span, often noticeable after a heavy snow winter.
  • Decks built without a permit, which can surface as a problem when the house is sold and the buyer's inspector asks for records.

If you recognize one of these on your deck, it is worth having it looked at before summer. Many can be fixed without a full rebuild; some cannot, and it is better to know.
